# Lesson 6: Ethical Implications — Bias, Fairness, and Transparency

## Overview

Ethical risk often appears inside ordinary work products rather than obvious crisis scenarios.

```text
AI-assisted output
      ↓
Affected people and decisions
      ↓
Bias, fairness, and transparency review
      ↓
Human verification, disclosure, or escalation
      ↓
Defensible ethical disposition
```

> Ethical review belongs in routine quality review whenever an output can affect people, opportunity, reputation, trust, or accountability.

This lesson develops five capabilities:

1. recognizing bias and fairness risk in framing and output;
2. identifying who is affected and how;
3. deciding when AI assistance should be disclosed;
4. documenting ethical reasoning when policy is incomplete; and
5. escalating when scale, harm, or authority exceeds individual judgment.

---

# Plain-English explanation

A document may be factually polished and still be ethically weak.

Examples include:

- a summary that consistently describes one group more negatively;
- a recommendation based on a biased framing;
- a hiring or evaluation draft that applies inconsistent standards;
- an audience-specific message that stereotypes its readers; or
- an AI-assisted document presented in a way that creates a false impression of fully human authorship.

```text
Accurate wording
      ≠
Fair treatment
      ≠
Ethical sufficiency
```

---

# One analogy: an uneven measuring scale

A scale may look precise while consistently favoring one side.

Likewise, an AI-assisted output may be fluent and structured while its framing, criteria, examples, or tone systematically advantages or disadvantages particular people.

The ethical review asks whether the measuring process itself is fair—not only whether the final numbers or sentences look reasonable.

---

# Bias can enter from several places

Bias is not limited to the model response.

## Prompt and task framing

Risk can enter through:

- leading questions;
- loaded descriptions;
- one-sided evidence;
- missing stakeholder perspectives;
- unequal evaluation criteria;
- labels that embed assumptions; and
- a request designed around a preferred conclusion.

```text
Biased framing
      ↓
Biased search space
      ↓
Biased output
```

## Source material

Approved sources may still contain:

- historical inequities;
- incomplete representation;
- inconsistent terminology;
- subjective manager notes;
- skewed samples; or
- decisions produced by earlier biased processes.

## Generated language

The output itself may introduce:

- unequal tone;
- stereotyped associations;
- unsupported inferences;
- stronger certainty for one group;
- different standards of evidence;
- omission of relevant context; or
- inconsistent recommendations.

---

# Fairness review

Fairness is not always identical treatment.

The review should ask whether relevantly similar cases are treated consistently and whether material differences are justified by the task rather than by stereotype or convenience.

## Core questions

- Who is affected?
- What decision, opportunity, burden, or communication could change?
- Which groups may experience different outcomes?
- Are the criteria relevant and consistently applied?
- Are protected, sensitive, or proxy characteristics influencing the result?
- Could framing or language create unequal treatment?
- Is there a correction, appeal, or recourse path?
- Who verifies the result before consequence?

```text
Same process for everyone
      ≠
Fair outcome by default
```

A process can apply the same flawed criteria consistently and still be unfair.

---

# Stakes increase the review burden

Ethical risk is highest when AI-assisted work affects:

- hiring, promotion, performance, or discipline;
- access to services, benefits, education, or credit;
- medical, legal, financial, or safety decisions;
- public reputation;
- surveillance or monitoring;
- vulnerable populations;
- high-volume communications; or
- people who cannot easily challenge the result.

```text
More people affected
+ greater consequence
+ weaker recourse
=
stronger ethical review and escalation need
```

---

# Transparency and disclosure

Disclosure requirements depend on:

- organizational policy;
- audience expectations;
- contractual or professional obligations;
- whether AI assistance is material;
- the risk of misleading authorship or expertise claims;
- whether the recipient may reasonably rely on a human-only process; and
- the consequences of concealment.

```text
AI used
      ≠
Always disclose in the same way
```

```text
No explicit disclosure rule found
      ≠
Concealment automatically appropriate
```

When uncertainty remains, the safer professional default is to disclose or seek authorized guidance rather than create a misleading impression.

## Useful disclosure qualities

A disclosure should be:

- accurate;
- proportionate;
- understandable to the audience;
- placed where it can be noticed;
- clear about the human review performed; and
- consistent with policy and professional obligations.

Example:

```text
AI assistance was used to prepare an initial draft.
The responsible manager reviewed and revised the final content.
```

---

# Structured ethical reasoning

When no single policy rule resolves the question, use a structured record.

## Six-part ethical review

1. **Affected parties** — Who may benefit, be burdened, or be excluded?
2. **Potential harm** — What could go wrong, and how severe or reversible is it?
3. **Fair outcome** — What would consistent and justified treatment look like?
4. **Transparency** — What should users, subjects, or recipients know?
5. **Human authority and recourse** — Who reviews, intervenes, explains, or hears appeals?
6. **Decision and escalation** — Can the team decide, or is specialist governance review required?

```text
Name affected parties
      ↓
Describe possible harm
      ↓
Define fair treatment
      ↓
Determine disclosure
      ↓
Define review and recourse
      ↓
Decide or escalate
```

The reasoning record matters because a conclusion without rationale is difficult to review, challenge, or improve.

---

# When individual reasoning is insufficient

Escalate when:

- the affected population is large;
- harm could be severe or difficult to reverse;
- protected or vulnerable groups may be affected;
- fairness criteria are contested or unclear;
- the team lacks subject-matter standing;
- the use changes eligibility, opportunity, or treatment;
- disclosure obligations are uncertain and material;
- the use could create surveillance or manipulation concerns;
- there is no meaningful recourse; or
- policy does not assign the decision to the current team.

```text
Structured reasoning completed
      ≠
Individual authority established
```

A strong escalation includes the facts, affected parties, analysis, open questions, proposed controls, and the point where the team’s authority ends.

---

# Worked example: performance-review summaries

A manager uses Claude to draft employee performance-review summaries from the manager’s own notes.

## Affected parties

- employees receiving the reviews;
- the manager;
- promotion, compensation, and talent-review stakeholders; and
- the organization relying on consistent evaluation.

## Possible harms

- generated wording introduces a harsher tone for some employees;
- subjective notes are amplified rather than questioned;
- similar performance is described using different standards;
- unsupported traits or motivations are inferred;
- employees cannot understand or challenge the basis; or
- AI assistance is concealed where policy or expectations require disclosure.

## Fair outcome

- each summary remains traceable to the manager’s actual notes;
- the same performance criteria are applied consistently;
- unsupported inferences are removed;
- tone and evidence standards are checked across employees;
- the manager owns the final assessment; and
- employees retain normal review or appeal channels.

## Human gate

- **Who:** The accountable manager, with HR or another qualified reviewer where required.
- **What:** Factual support, consistency, bias, tone, policy compliance, and unsupported inference.
- **When:** Before the review is finalized, shared, or used in a compensation or promotion decision.

## Classification

```text
Appropriate with human review
+ fairness check
+ manager accountability
+ disclosure according to policy and context
```

The defensibility comes from the reasoning and gate—not from the label alone.

# Common failure modes

| Failure | Repair |
|---|---|
| Ethical review treated as optional | Add it to routine review for people-facing and consequential work |
| Bias checked only in final wording | Review prompt, sources, criteria, and framing |
| Same process assumed automatically fair | Examine effects, justification, and recourse |
| AI disclosure hidden or improvised | Apply policy, audience, and materiality criteria |
| Human reviewer lacks authority | Define a qualified pre-use gate |
| Team decides beyond its standing | Escalate with documented reasoning |
| Ethical reasoning has no record | Preserve affected parties, harms, fairness standard, disclosure, and disposition |
| High-volume use approved from one example | Test representative groups and monitor outcomes |

---

# Ethical review protocol

```text
1. Define the use case and decision context
2. Identify affected parties and power relationships
3. Inspect prompt, sources, criteria, and framing for bias
4. Assess possible unequal treatment or disparate effects
5. Define what a fair outcome requires
6. Determine transparency and disclosure obligations
7. Define human review, intervention, explanation, and recourse
8. Test representative and edge cases
9. Document reasoning and residual uncertainty
10. Escalate when scale, harm, standing, or policy requires it
11. Approve, constrain, redesign, defer, or reject
12. Monitor outcomes and revisit after material change
```

---

# Exam lens

```text
People-facing output                       → review bias, fairness, and transparency
Same criteria but unequal effects          → fairness risk remains
Biased prompt or evidence                  → correct upstream framing
Disclosure requirement unclear             → seek guidance or disclose rather than conceal
Reviewer lacks standing                    → escalate
Large affected population                  → stronger testing and governance review
No appeal or correction path               → add recourse or redesign
Policy-compliant but unfair outcome         → ethics review still required
Performance-review draft                    → human-owned, fairness-checked, pre-use review
```

For ethical-implication scenarios:

1. identify who is affected;
2. inspect framing, sources, and generated language;
3. assess stakes, distribution of harm, and recourse;
4. define a justified fairness standard;
5. determine disclosure expectations;
6. preserve accountable human ownership;
7. document the reasoning;
8. test representative cases;
9. escalate beyond the team’s standing; and
10. monitor for actual unequal outcomes.

---

# Short recap

```text
1. Ethical risk can hide in ordinary outputs.
2. Bias may enter through prompts, sources, criteria, or generated language.
3. Fairness requires justified and consistent treatment, not identical wording alone.
4. Disclosure depends on context, policy, materiality, and audience expectations.
5. Ambiguous cases require documented reasoning.
6. Scale, severe harm, vulnerable groups, or weak authority require escalation.
7. Human review must occur before consequence and include intervention rights.
8. Policy compliance does not automatically establish ethical sufficiency.
```
