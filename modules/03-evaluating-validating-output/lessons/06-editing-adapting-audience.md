# Lesson 6: Editing and Adapting Output for Your Audience

## Overview

Claude can produce a useful first draft. A first draft is not automatically a finished deliverable.

The editing pass is where professional judgment is applied to:

- clarity;
- tone;
- structure;
- audience needs;
- confidentiality;
- uncertainty;
- decision relevance; and
- organizational standards.

> Claude drafts; the accountable human delivers.

A strong editing process preserves the verified substance while changing how that substance is selected, ordered, explained, and formatted for a particular reader.

This lesson develops five capabilities:

1. converting a raw draft into a professional deliverable;
2. applying clarity, tone, and formatting passes separately;
3. adapting one verified analysis for several audiences;
4. comparing candidate drafts before selecting a base; and
5. checking that adaptation did not distort facts, uncertainty, or obligations.

---

# Plain-English explanation

An accurate draft can still be hard to use.

It may be:

- too long;
- too vague;
- too technical;
- too formal;
- poorly organized;
- missing the decision point;
- inappropriate for the relationship; or
- unsafe for the intended audience.

Editing asks:

```text
What does this reader need to understand?
          ↓
What decision or action should the output support?
          ↓
Which verified facts must remain visible?
          ↓
How much detail, explanation, and context does this audience need?
          ↓
What tone and format make the information usable?
```

The underlying facts should remain stable. The presentation changes.

```text
Facts, evidence, uncertainty, and obligations → remain invariant
Selection, depth, tone, order, and format     → adapt to the audience
```

---

# One analogy: tailoring the same suit

Imagine a tailor starts with one piece of verified fabric.

The fabric does not change into a different material merely because the wearer changes. But the cut, fit, length, and styling are adjusted for the person and occasion.

Audience adaptation works the same way:

- the **fabric** is the verified evidence and facts;
- the **measurements** are the audience's knowledge, needs, and constraints;
- the **cut** is the selection and ordering of information;
- the **style** is the tone and level of formality; and
- the **final fitting** is the review that confirms nothing important was distorted or exposed.

A suit cut for a formal ceremony is not the same as one cut for everyday work, even when both use the same fabric.

> Good editing changes the fit, not the truth.

---

# From raw output to deliverable

Use three distinct editing passes.

```text
Verified draft
      ↓
Clarity pass
      ↓
Tone pass
      ↓
Formatting pass
      ↓
Audience and integrity review
      ↓
Finished deliverable
```

Separating the passes makes defects easier to diagnose. A sentence can be clear but use the wrong tone. A document can have an appropriate tone but be difficult to scan.

---

# Pass 1: Clarity

Clarity makes the meaning easier to understand without changing it.

## Clarity actions

- remove repetition;
- shorten loose sentences;
- replace vague references;
- state the main point earlier;
- separate facts from interpretation;
- preserve units, dates, conditions, and owners;
- replace unnecessary jargon;
- define necessary technical terms;
- remove decorative language; and
- make actions and decisions explicit.

## Claude's common drafting tendency

Claude often favors completeness and smooth transitions. This can produce:

- long introductions;
- repeated conclusions;
- excessive qualification;
- several sentences where one would work;
- abstract language instead of a direct action; and
- buried recommendations.

Editing should not automatically remove every qualification. Some caveats are evidence, not clutter.

### Before

```text
The analysis appears to indicate that there may be an opportunity for the organization to consider taking steps to improve the consistency of the review process.
```

### Clearer

```text
Standardize the review process to improve consistency.
```

The revision preserves the recommendation while removing empty hedging.

### Dangerous over-edit

```text
The review process is inconsistent and must be replaced immediately.
```

This version changes the strength and urgency of the claim. Clarity is not permission to increase certainty beyond the evidence.

---

# Pass 2: Tone

Tone is the relationship between the content, reader, and occasion.

The same facts may need different registers.

| Audience | Typical tone |
|---|---|
| Peer | Direct, collaborative, technically honest |
| Working team | Operational, specific, action oriented |
| Executive | Concise, decision focused, impact oriented |
| Client | Professional, clear, controlled, accountable |
| Public audience | Accessible, neutral, disclosure aware |
| Regulator, auditor, or legal reviewer | Precise, formal, evidence based, qualified |

## Tone dimensions

- formal versus conversational;
- direct versus diplomatic;
- technical versus accessible;
- urgent versus measured;
- confident versus appropriately qualified;
- persuasive versus neutral; and
- collaborative versus authoritative.

## Tone must not distort evidence

A more confident tone cannot convert an uncertain finding into a fact.

A more diplomatic tone cannot conceal a material risk.

A more persuasive tone cannot remove counterevidence.

```text
Tone adaptation
      ≠
Evidence manipulation
```

---

# Pass 3: Formatting

Formatting shapes how the audience consumes the information.

## Executive readers

Often benefit from:

- decision first;
- impact and risk near the top;
- a short recommendation;
- a small number of key metrics;
- clear asks or approvals; and
- supporting detail moved below or into an appendix.

## Working teams

Often need:

- methods and assumptions;
- dependencies;
- owners;
- dates;
- task status;
- decision logs;
- unresolved questions; and
- next actions.

## External recipients

Often require:

- controlled disclosure;
- polished structure;
- approved terminology;
- consistent organizational voice;
- appropriate caveats;
- source support where necessary; and
- removal of internal-only commentary.

## Review and audit audiences

Often need:

- traceable claims;
- source locations;
- calculation methods;
- exceptions;
- uncertainty;
- version information; and
- approval records.

> Format should expose what the audience needs to inspect, not hide what is inconvenient.

---

# The audience contract

Before adapting a draft, define the audience contract.

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who will read or hear this? |
| Expertise | What does the audience already understand? |
| Purpose | What should the communication accomplish? |
| Decision | What decision, action, or response should follow? |
| Stakes | What happens if the message is misunderstood? |
| Time | How much attention will the audience give it? |
| Format | Where and how will it be consumed? |
| Disclosure | What may or may not be shared? |
| Tone | What relationship and occasion apply? |
| Evidence | Which facts, caveats, and sources must remain visible? |

Without this contract, `make it executive-friendly` or `make it professional` is too vague to guide reliable adaptation.

---

# Truth-preserving adaptation

Audience adaptation has two categories of content.

## Invariants

These should not change across versions unless the evidence changes:

- factual claims;
- numbers and units;
- dates;
- source-supported conclusions;
- confidence and uncertainty;
- material risks;
- legal, regulatory, contractual, or policy obligations;
- dependencies and conditions; and
- approved organizational positions.

## Adaptable elements

These may change for the audience:

- order;
- length;
- vocabulary;
- amount of method detail;
- examples;
- tone;
- headings;
- visual structure;
- level of background explanation; and
- placement of supporting evidence.

```text
Verified content model
          ↓
Audience-specific selection and presentation
          ↓
Invariant check
```

---

# Selective omission versus misleading omission

A shorter version must omit some detail. The question is whether the omitted detail is safe to remove for the intended use.

## Appropriate selective omission

An executive summary may move detailed methodology to an appendix when:

- the main conclusion remains accurate;
- material assumptions are disclosed;
- important risks remain visible;
- the detailed method remains accessible; and
- the summary is not presented as more certain than the analysis.

## Misleading omission

Adaptation becomes unsafe when it removes:

- a condition that changes the conclusion;
- a major risk;
- conflicting evidence;
- an important dependency;
- uncertainty that affects the decision;
- the denominator or time period for a metric;
- the reason a recommendation is provisional; or
- information required by policy or professional standards.

> Brevity is not a justification for hiding decision-critical context.

---

# One analysis, several audiences

A single verified analysis may generate several deliverables.

| Audience | Lead with | Preserve | Reduce or relocate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive | Decision, impact, risk, requested action | Key evidence, uncertainty, implications | Detailed method and working notes |
| Working team | Tasks, causes, dependencies, owners, dates | Method, evidence, open questions | High-level background already known |
| Client | Outcome, implications, commitments, next steps | Supported facts, approved caveats, accountability | Internal debate and nonapproved content |
| Regulator or auditor | Requirement, evidence, exception, control, approval | Traceability, methodology, limitations | Marketing language and unnecessary persuasion |
| Public | Essential facts, impact, action, accessible context | Accuracy, approved disclosure, uncertainty | Confidential detail and internal jargon |

The facts remain the same. The information architecture changes.

# Comparing candidates before editing

When quality matters, generate or obtain more than one candidate before committing to a base draft.

Sources of candidates may include:

- separate runs of the same prompt;
- prompts with different structures;
- different model tiers or systems;
- independent human drafts; or
- alternative framing strategies.

## Candidate comparison workflow

```text
Generate bounded candidates
          ↓
Verify common factual foundation
          ↓
Score against the same criteria
          ↓
Select the strongest base
          ↓
Combine only compatible strengths
          ↓
Edit and revalidate
```

## Comparison criteria

- accuracy and evidence support;
- completeness;
- audience fit;
- clarity;
- structure;
- tone;
- risk and caveat preservation;
- actionability;
- disclosure control; and
- amount of editing required.

## Important boundary

```text
Best-looking candidate
      ≠
Most accurate candidate
```

Candidate comparison helps select a draft. It does not replace grounding, fact-checking, or human review.

Do not merge attractive sentences from several candidates without checking whether their assumptions and conclusions are compatible.

# Practical example: service-response analysis

## Verified source analysis

A team analyzes service-request data and confirms:

- median resolution time increased from 20 hours to 24 hours during the quarter;
- request volume increased 11%;
- two newly hired analysts were still in training;
- the evidence supports association, not a precise causal allocation;
- a documented triage checklist is incomplete; and
- the team must propose an owner and date for improving the checklist.

## Raw draft

```text
The analysis shows that median resolution time increased from 20 hours to 24 hours during the quarter, which represents a 20% increase, and this may have been influenced by an 11% increase in request volume as well as the fact that two recently hired analysts were undergoing training during the period. The existing triage checklist is also incomplete, and it may be beneficial for the team to consider updating it and assigning appropriate ownership and timelines for doing so.
```

The draft is broadly accurate, but it is not optimized for any audience.

## Executive version

```text
Median resolution time rose 20%, from 20 to 24 hours, while request volume increased 11% and two new analysts remained in training. Approve an owner and deadline to complete the triage checklist; the current evidence does not isolate the contribution of each driver.
```

### Why it works

- leads with the metric;
- states impact and context;
- contains the decision request;
- preserves uncertainty about causation; and
- removes unnecessary process detail.

## Working-team version

```text
Quarter result: median resolution time increased from 20 to 24 hours (+20%). Relevant conditions were an 11% volume increase, two analysts still in training, and an incomplete triage checklist. We cannot assign a precise causal share to each factor from the current data. At the next standup, assign an owner and target date for completing and testing the checklist.
```

### Why it works

- preserves the data and limitation;
- separates conditions from causal proof;
- identifies the operational action; and
- specifies where ownership will be assigned.

## External client version

```text
Median resolution time increased from 20 to 24 hours during the quarter. We are updating the triage checklist and strengthening onboarding support while continuing to monitor volume and resolution performance. We will provide the agreed progress update at the next service review.
```

### Why it works

- communicates the verified performance change;
- states the response without exposing internal speculation;
- avoids claiming unsupported causation; and
- aligns to an external accountability context.

### Required review

The external version still requires review for:

- contractual reporting obligations;
- approved commitments;
- client-specific disclosure requirements;
- accuracy of the promised update; and
- accountable-owner approval.

# The editing and adaptation protocol

```text
1. Verify the source draft
          ↓
2. Define the audience contract
          ↓
3. Identify invariant facts, uncertainty, and obligations
          ↓
4. Select the appropriate information depth
          ↓
5. Apply clarity, tone, and formatting passes
          ↓
6. Compare candidate versions when quality warrants it
          ↓
7. Check for distortion, omission, disclosure, and inconsistency
          ↓
8. Obtain required review and approval
          ↓
9. Release in the appropriate channel and format
```

## Step 1: Verify before adapting

Do not polish unsupported content. Confirm the factual foundation, source support, calculations, and coverage first.

## Step 2: Define the audience contract

Document reader, purpose, decision, expertise, time, tone, format, disclosure, and stakes.

## Step 3: Mark invariants

Create a short list of facts, caveats, obligations, and risks that must survive every version.

## Step 4: Choose depth

Decide which information belongs in the main message, supporting detail, appendix, or internal working notes.

## Step 5: Edit in separate passes

Run clarity, tone, and formatting reviews separately.

## Step 6: Compare candidates

Score alternatives against common criteria rather than choosing by preference alone.

## Step 7: Revalidate

Compare each audience version with the verified content model.

## Step 8: Review and approve

Apply the Diligence thresholds and review gates from the previous lesson.

# Paste-ready prompts

## Audience-contract prompt

```text
Before rewriting, define the audience contract for this communication:
- audience and expertise;
- purpose;
- decision or action expected;
- attention available;
- tone and relationship;
- required format;
- disclosure restrictions;
- material facts, uncertainty, risks, and obligations that must remain visible.

Do not draft until the contract is explicit.
```

## Truth-preserving adaptation prompt

```text
Adapt the verified source content for [AUDIENCE] and [PURPOSE].

Preserve exactly:
- all material facts and figures;
- units, dates, and conditions;
- uncertainty and confidence level;
- material risks and dependencies;
- required caveats and obligations; and
- approved organizational positions.

You may change:
- order;
- length;
- vocabulary;
- amount of background and method detail;
- tone; and
- formatting.

After drafting, provide an invariant-check table showing where each required fact or caveat appears.
```

## Candidate-comparison prompt

```text
Compare the candidate drafts against the same criteria:
1. factual fidelity;
2. evidence and caveat preservation;
3. completeness for the intended audience;
4. clarity;
5. tone;
6. structure;
7. actionability;
8. disclosure control; and
9. editing effort required.

Do not select by style alone. Identify incompatible assumptions before combining material from different candidates.
```

## Executive adaptation prompt

```text
Create an executive version that leads with the decision, impact, risk, and requested action. Keep the main body concise, but preserve any uncertainty, dependency, or condition that could change the decision. Move supporting method detail to a clearly labeled appendix section rather than deleting it.
```

## Working-team adaptation prompt

```text
Create a working-team version that preserves the evidence, method, dependencies, open questions, owners, dates, and next actions. Use operational headings and distinguish facts, hypotheses, and unresolved issues.
```

# Audience-integrity checklist

Before release, check:

## Factual fidelity

- [ ] Numbers, units, dates, and names match the verified source.
- [ ] Causal language does not exceed the evidence.
- [ ] Uncertainty remains visible.
- [ ] Material risks and dependencies are retained.
- [ ] No new commitments were introduced.

## Audience fit

- [ ] The message begins with what this audience needs most.
- [ ] Vocabulary fits the reader's expertise.
- [ ] The level of detail fits the available attention.
- [ ] The expected decision or action is clear.
- [ ] Tone fits the relationship and occasion.

## Disclosure

- [ ] Confidential or internal-only content is removed from external versions.
- [ ] Required disclosures and caveats remain.
- [ ] The recipient is authorized to receive the information.
- [ ] Public or external wording reflects approved organizational positions.

## Format

- [ ] The structure supports scanning and comprehension.
- [ ] Headings and labels are informative.
- [ ] Supporting detail is available where needed.
- [ ] Tables, bullets, or narrative are used intentionally.
- [ ] Accessibility requirements are considered.

## Approval

- [ ] Required reviewer and approver have completed the gate.
- [ ] The final version is the version that was reviewed.
- [ ] The release channel and recipient list are correct.

# Common anti-patterns

## Anti-pattern 1: Polishing before verification

**Failure:** Unsupported content becomes more persuasive.

**Repair:** Verify the factual base before editing for style.

## Anti-pattern 2: Treating shorter as better

**Failure:** Decision-critical conditions or uncertainty disappear.

**Repair:** Use selective omission with an invariant checklist.

## Anti-pattern 3: Making the tone more confident

**Failure:** Style increases certainty beyond the evidence.

**Repair:** Preserve the original confidence level and support status.

## Anti-pattern 4: One version for every audience

**Failure:** Executives are buried in method, while working teams lack operational detail.

**Repair:** Create audience-specific versions from one verified content model.

## Anti-pattern 5: Hiding risk in an appendix

**Failure:** The main message becomes misleading.

**Repair:** Keep decision-changing risks and conditions in the main communication.

## Anti-pattern 6: Choosing the most polished candidate

**Failure:** Style outranks factual fidelity and completeness.

**Repair:** Compare candidates against explicit criteria.

## Anti-pattern 7: Combining drafts without reconciliation

**Failure:** Incompatible assumptions, metrics, or conclusions are merged.

**Repair:** Reconcile candidates to the same source and content model first.

## Anti-pattern 8: Externalizing internal speculation

**Failure:** Unverified internal hypotheses appear as client-facing explanations.

**Repair:** Separate verified facts, internal hypotheses, and approved external messaging.

## Anti-pattern 9: Adaptation without disclosure review

**Failure:** Sensitive or internal content reaches an unauthorized audience.

**Repair:** Include disclosure classification in the audience contract.

# Exam reasoning pattern

For editing and audience-adaptation scenarios:

1. verify the factual base before polishing;
2. identify the audience, purpose, decision, expertise, and channel;
3. preserve facts, figures, conditions, uncertainty, risks, and obligations;
4. adapt selection, depth, tone, order, and format;
5. distinguish appropriate brevity from misleading omission;
6. keep decision-changing information visible;
7. compare multiple candidates against common criteria when quality matters;
8. avoid choosing by fluency or style alone;
9. review disclosure and audience authorization;
10. apply required human-review and approval gates; and
11. validate that the final version matches the reviewed version.

```text
Accurate but verbose          → clarity pass
Accurate but wrong register   → tone pass
Accurate but hard to consume  → formatting pass
Same analysis, new audience   → preserve invariants and adapt depth
Several candidate drafts      → compare against common criteria
Short version drops a caveat  → restore decision-critical context
External version exposes internals → disclosure review
```

# Knowledge check

## Question 1

Why is an accurate draft not necessarily finished?

**Answer:** It may still be unclear, poorly structured, inappropriate in tone, incomplete for the audience, or unsafe for the intended channel.

## Question 2

What must remain invariant across audience versions?

**Answer:** Material facts, numbers, dates, source-supported conclusions, uncertainty, risks, dependencies, obligations, and approved positions.

## Question 3

What may change across versions?

**Answer:** Selection, order, length, vocabulary, level of detail, examples, tone, headings, and formatting.

## Question 4

What is the difference between selective omission and misleading omission?

**Answer:** Selective omission removes detail that is unnecessary for the intended use while preserving decision-critical context. Misleading omission removes information that changes interpretation or action.

## Question 5

Why compare candidates before editing?

**Answer:** Comparing bounded alternatives can reveal stronger structures and reduce the cost of rescuing a weak draft, provided all candidates are checked against the same evidence and criteria.

## Question 6

Does the most polished candidate deserve selection?

**Answer:** Not automatically. Factual fidelity, completeness, uncertainty preservation, audience fit, and disclosure control are more important than surface style.

## Question 7

Why must external adaptations receive disclosure review?

**Answer:** They may expose internal reasoning, confidential information, unapproved commitments, or statements that create reliance.

## Question 8

What should happen after adaptation?

**Answer:** Run an invariant and disclosure check, apply required review gates, and confirm that the released version is the reviewed version.
